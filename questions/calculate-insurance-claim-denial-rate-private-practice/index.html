How do I calculate my practice's insurance claim denial rate?

Count the claims you submitted in a period, count how many of them were denied, and divide. For example, 12 denied out of 200 submitted is a 6% denial rate. Keep the denial reason on the row even after you resubmit, or the rate will look better than it was. Then run the same count by payer and by denial reason. That's the part that tells you what to fix.

How to work it out

  1. Log every claim when you submit it, with the date submitted, payer and CPT code. A claim that isn't in the log can't be counted.
  2. Record a denial reason when a claim comes back denied, from a short fixed list such as eligibility, missing authorization, timely filing, coding, duplicate or missing information.
  3. Keep the reason after you resubmit. Change the status, but leave the reason, so the claim still counts as denied once.
  4. Divide denied claims by submitted claims for the same period. Use the same period every time, such as a month or a quarter, so the numbers compare.
  5. Count by payer and by reason too. One payer that denies far more than the rest, or one reason that keeps coming back, is where to start.
  6. Watch the trend, not one month. With a small caseload, one or two denials move the percentage a lot.

What gets denied, and how resubmissions and appeals work, depends on your payers, plans and contracts. Check your payer's provider manual. This is record-keeping guidance, not billing advice.

Common follow-up questions

What's a typical denial rate for a small therapy practice?

We don't quote a benchmark, because published figures mix very different payers, specialties and claim types. Your own rate over time, and how your payers compare with each other, is the more useful number. If it climbs, look at the reasons first.

Should a claim that was denied twice count twice?

Count it once in the denial rate, since it's one claim. If you want to see how much rework you're doing, keep a separate count of resubmissions.

Does an underpaid claim count as a denial?

Not in this count. A claim paid below the expected amount is an underpayment. Track it on its own, since the fix is different.
