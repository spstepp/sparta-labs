How do I get my private practice books ready for my CPA at tax time?

Before you meet your CPA, get four things in order: income received for the year, split by where it came from; business expenses grouped by category; what you set aside and paid toward estimated taxes; and a year that matches your bank statement. If you log these as you go, year-end is a check, not a project. Ask your CPA for their own list too, since what they need depends on your situation.

How to do it

  1. Ask your CPA what they want and in what format. Some want totals, some want the detailed rows, and most have a checklist.
  2. Finish logging income for the year, using the date each payment arrived. Flag insurance payments for December sessions that show up in January, and ask your CPA which year they belong to.
  3. Find expenses with no category and fix them, so every row sits in a group your CPA can read.
  4. Match the year to your bank statement. If the December ending balance doesn't line up, find the gap now, not in March.
  5. Total what you set aside and what you paid toward estimated taxes for each period, with the dates you paid.
  6. Save a copy of the finished year and start a fresh file for January, so next year's numbers don't mix in.

This is record-keeping guidance, not tax advice. What your CPA needs, which year a payment belongs to and how long to keep records depend on your situation, so check with a CPA or tax professional.

Common follow-up questions

Do I still need a CPA if I keep a spreadsheet?

A spreadsheet keeps your records. It doesn't do your taxes. Many solo therapists hand the year's totals to a CPA or tax preparer, but whether you need one depends on your situation.

What if I didn't track anything this year?

Start with your bank and card statements and your EHR or billing reports. Sort the deposits and business charges into income and expense categories, then give those totals to your CPA. It takes longer, but it works.

Should I keep the detailed records after I file?

Yes. Ask your CPA how long to keep them; the IRS also publishes guidance on record retention.
