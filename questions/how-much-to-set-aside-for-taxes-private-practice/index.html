How much should a self-employed therapist set aside for taxes?

There isn't one right percentage. As a solo practice owner you owe self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) on your net profit, plus federal income tax and usually state income tax, and the mix depends on your income, your state and the rest of your household's income. A practical way to land on a number: divide last year's total tax by last year's practice profit, adjust for how this year is going, and have your CPA check it. Then set that share aside from profit, not from gross deposits.

How to work out your number

  1. Start from profit, not deposits. Tax is figured on income minus business expenses, so a rate applied to every deposit will run high if you have real costs like rent, insurance or an EHR.
  2. Know what the money has to cover. The IRS self-employment tax rate is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security and 2.9% Medicare) on net earnings from self-employment. Federal income tax and state tax come on top, and the Social Security part stops above a yearly earnings cap.
  3. Use last year's return as a starting point. Add up the federal and state tax you owed for the year, divide by your practice profit, and you have a rough rate. The IRS suggests the prior year's return as a guide for estimating too.
  4. Adjust for what changed. A bigger caseload, a new office lease, a spouse's job change or a move to another state can all move the number. If you're in your first year, you don't have a past return, so ask your CPA for a starting rate.
  5. Set the money aside each time profit comes in, or at least monthly, in a separate savings account. Waiting until the due date is where most shortfalls start.
  6. Check it every quarter. Compare what you've set aside with your profit so far. If income jumped, raise the rate before the next payment, not in April.

This is record-keeping guidance, not tax advice. Your actual estimated payments depend on income tax brackets, other household income, deductions, state rules and safe-harbor rules, so get your set-aside rate from a CPA or tax professional. Rates and limits are on IRS.gov: Self-employment tax and Estimated taxes.

Common follow-up questions

Is setting money aside the same as paying estimated taxes?

No. Setting money aside just keeps it from getting spent. You still have to send estimated payments to the IRS, and to your state if it requires them, by each due date.

What if I set aside too much?

The extra stays in your savings account, or covers what's due when you file. Many solo practitioners would rather be a little over than short. Your CPA can tell you if your rate is far off.

Is there a way to avoid an underpayment penalty?

The IRS says most people avoid it if they owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits, or if they paid at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's, whichever is smaller. Higher-income taxpayers have different rules, so check Publication 505 or ask your CPA.
